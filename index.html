1. Present Simple vs. Present Continuous

The two present tenses do completely different jobs. Turkish has one present tense form that covers both meanings, so this is the single most common source of error for Turkish speakers. The difference is not about time — it is about permanent vs. temporary and fact vs. action-in-progress.

Present SimplePresent Continuous
Form: base verb (+ -s for he/she/it)
I work / he works / they work

Negative: do/does + not + base
I don't work. She doesn't work.

Question: Do/Does + subject + base?
Do you work? Does she work? 		Form: am/is/are + verb-ing
I am working / he is working / they are working

Negative: am/is/are + not + verb-ing
I'm not working. She isn't working.

Question: Am/Is/Are + subject + verb-ing?
Are you working? Is she working?

2. Question Tags

A question tag is a short question added to the end of a statement. Turkish uses "değil mi?" for almost everything. English is much more precise — the tag must match the auxiliary verb and the subject of the main sentence.

StatementTagWhy + More Examples
You're always on TikTok, aren't you? Positive statement → negative tag. are → aren't.
She's your sister, isn't she?
They're coming tonight, aren't they?
We're late, aren't we?
She doesn't post much, does she? Negative statement → positive tag.
He doesn't like coffee, does he?
You don't watch TV, do you?
They don't live here, do they?
They've deleted the app, haven't they? The auxiliary is have, so the tag uses haven't.
You've finished, haven't you?
She's already left, hasn't she?
We've met before, haven't we?
He can edit videos, can't he? Modal can → can't.
You can swim, can't you?
She can drive, can't she?
They can stay, can't they?
We should log off, shouldn't we? Modal should → shouldn't.
I should apologise, shouldn't I?
He should try harder, shouldn't he?
I'm the only one here, aren't I? Special case. Never "amn't I".
I'm late, aren't I?
I'm next, aren't I?
Nobody called me, did they? Nobody → they, and the statement is negative, so the tag is positive.
Nobody knows, do they?
Nothing happened, did it?
No one came, did they?
Let's delete the app, shall we? Fixed form. Memorise it.
Let's go, shall we?
Let's start, shall we?
Open the window, will you? Imperatives take will you (or won't you for invitations).
Sit down, will you?
Have some tea, won't you?
There's a problem, isn't there? The tag matches there is.
There are two left, aren't there?
There isn't any milk, is there?
💡 Intonation matters — this is real speaking.
• Falling intonation = you are sure, you just want agreement: "Nice weather, isn't it?"
• Rising intonation = you are genuinely asking: "You didn't tell her, did you?"

Compare:
"You're Turkish, aren't you?" ↘ (I'm almost certain.)
"You're Turkish, aren't you?" ↗ (I'm really not sure — please confirm.)
🇹🇷 Common Turkish-speaker errors:
❌ You love gaming, do you? → ✅ You love gaming, don't you?
❌ Your friends are online, isn't it? → ✅ Your friends are online, aren't they?
❌ She is your sister, isn't she is? → ✅ She is your sister, isn't she?
❌ You have a car, haven't you? (British only) → In American English: You have a car, don't you?

3. Comparatives — Beyond the Basics

You already know bigger, more interesting, the best. At 9th grade, you need the six structures below. They are what separates B1 from B2 writing and speaking.

StructureMeaningExamples
as … asEqual Instagram is as addictive as TikTok.
My brother is as tall as my father.
This exam wasn't as hard as I expected.
Turkish coffee is as strong as espresso.
not as … asLess than Twitter isn't as popular as it used to be.
My phone isn't as fast as yours.
He's not as confident as he looks.
This film isn't as interesting as the book.
This exam was not as difficult as the last one.
the more … the moreParallel increase The more you scroll, the more tired you feel.
The more I study, the more I understand.
The older I get, the less I care about trends.
The more she practises, the better she gets.
The more you sleep, the more energy you have.
… and … (double comparative)Continuous change My feed is getting stranger and stranger.
The weather is getting colder and colder.
Prices are rising higher and higher.
She's becoming more and more confident.
He's playing better and better every week.
far / much / slightly / a bit + comparativeEmphasis or moderation This app is far more distracting than that one.
She's much taller than me.
I'm slightly older than him.
This one is a bit cheaper.
Today is far worse than yesterday.
the … of the twoChoice between exactly two She's the more honest of the two friends.
This is the better of the two options.
He's the taller of the two brothers.
Which is the more useful of the two apps?
💡 Spelling rules for short comparatives
• One syllable → +er / +est: tall → taller → tallest · fast → faster → fastest
• Ends in -e → just +r / +st: nice → nicer → nicest · large → larger → largest
• Ends in consonant + y → change y to i: happy → happier → happiest · easy → easier → easiest
• CVC → double the last letter: big → bigger → biggest · hot → hotter → hottest
• Two syllables or more → more / most: interesting → more interesting → most interesting
• Irregular: good → better → best · bad → worse → worst · far → further/farther → furthest/farthest · little → less → least · much/many → more → most
🇹🇷 Common Turkish-speaker errors:
❌ More better → ✅ better (never double the comparative)
❌ Addictiver → ✅ more addictive (three syllables takes more)
❌ More easier → ✅ easier
❌ This app isn't so useful than the other one. → ✅ This app isn't as useful as the other one.
❌ More you compare, worse you feel. → ✅ The more you compare, the worse you feel.
❌ She is more tall than me. → ✅ She is taller than me.

Every time you open a social media app, you are not opening a window onto the world. You are opening a window onto a world that has been carefully built for you — by a machine. That machine is called a recommendation algorithm, and its job is not to inform you or entertain you. Its job is to keep you there.

The system watches everything. It notices that you pause for 2.3 seconds longer on videos about basketball. It notices that you scroll straight past posts about politics. It notices what time you go to bed and what time you wake up. Then it uses all of this information to build a version of you — a mathematical model that predicts, with unnerving accuracy, what you will click on next.

The problem is that what keeps you scrolling is not the same as what makes you happy. Outrage keeps people engaged. Fear keeps people engaged. Comparison keeps people engaged. A feed that made you feel calm and satisfied would be a feed you would close — and a feed you close is a feed that makes no money.

This creates what researchers call an echo chamber. If you show mild interest in one idea, the algorithm floods you with it. Soon your entire feed agrees with you, and you begin to assume that everyone in the world agrees with you too. The effect is subtle but powerful: you stop encountering people who disagree, and you slowly lose the ability to argue with them.

There is a second, stranger consequence. Because the algorithm shows you the same twenty creators over and over, you begin to feel that you know them. You know their bedroom, their dog, their opinions on pineapple on pizza. Psychologists call this a parasocial relationship — a one-sided bond in which you feel intimacy that the other person does not feel and does not know exists. It is not a mental illness and it is not stupid. It is simply what happens when a machine is designed to simulate friendship.

None of this means you should delete everything and live in a forest. It means something much simpler: you should know what you are looking at. The next time your feed shows you something that makes you furious, ask yourself a boring but useful question — did I choose this, or was it chosen for me?

1. Three Past Tenses — The Storytelling Toolkit

Turkish has one past tense form that covers most of what English splits into three. When you tell a story in English, you need to keep two or three of them running at once. This is the single most important skill for Week 2.

TenseFormFunctionExamples
Past Continuous was/were + verb-ing Sets the background scene. Describes an action in progress in the past. I was practising my aim when everything changed.
She was walking home when it started raining.
They were watching TV at 9 p.m. last night.
We were eating dinner when the phone rang.
Past Simple verb-ed or irregular The main events, in order. The things that actually happened. I joined the team, lost five matches, and nearly quit.
He scored, celebrated, and hugged his coach.
We arrived at 8, ate quickly, and left by 9.
She called me, said sorry, and hung up.
Past Perfect had + past participle An action that happened BEFORE another past action. The "past of the past." By the time I arrived, the coach had already left.
She had never seen the sea before that summer.
When I got to the cinema, the film had already started.
He told me he had finished the project two days earlier.
💡 Signal words that tell you which tense to use
Past Continuous: while, as, when (for the interrupting action), at that moment, at 8 p.m. yesterday
Past Simple: then, next, after that, finally, yesterday, last week, in 2019, two days ago
Past Perfect: already, before, by the time, never (before), when
💡 The classic storytelling pattern — while + Past Continuous, when + Past Simple
While I was warming up, my controller disconnected.
I was walking to school when I saw the accident.
She was cooking dinner when the power went out.
They were arguing when the teacher walked in.
The while clause sets the scene. The when clause delivers the event.
💡 Past Perfect — the "already happened first" tense
Compare these two sentences:
When I arrived, she left. (I arrived, then she left.)
When I arrived, she had left. (She left first, then I arrived — and she was gone.)

More examples:
He had never eaten sushi before that trip.
By the time we got there, they had sold all the tickets.
I realised I had forgotten my keys at home.
🇹🇷 Common Turkish-speaker errors:
❌ When I arrived at the stadium, the match already started. → ✅ …the match had already started.
❌ I was knowing him for years. → ✅ I had known him for years.
❌ While I played, he called me. → ✅ While I was playing, he called me.
❌ I had went there. → ✅ I had gone there. (Past Perfect uses V3, not V2)
❌ I have seen him yesterday. → ✅ I saw him yesterday.

2. Modals of Deduction — Guessing About the Past

When you don't know what happened but you have evidence, you speculate. English does this with modal + have + past participle. This is enormously useful in speaking because it lets you talk about things you didn't witness.

ModalCertaintyMeaningExamples
must have + V3 ~95% sure it happened Strong logical conclusion He must have trained for years — look at his accuracy.
She must have been exhausted after the marathon.
They must have left early — the lights are off.
You must have been terrified!
may have / might have / could have + V3 ~50% — possible You genuinely don't know She might have forgotten the match.
He may have missed the train.
They could have gone to the wrong station.
I might have left my keys in the car.
can't have + V3 ~95% sure it did NOT happen Logically impossible They can't have won — they're bottom of the league.
He can't have finished already — it's only been ten minutes.
She can't have said that — it's not like her.
You can't have seen him — he's in Berlin.
⚠ Not mustn't have. For a negative deduction, English uses can't have, not mustn't have.
❌ He mustn't have trained. → ✅ He can't have trained.
❌ She mustn't have known. → ✅ She can't have known.
💡 Deduction about the PRESENT is different — no have.
Present: He must be exhausted. (I'm sure he is exhausted now.)
Present: She must be at work.
Present: They can't be serious.
Present: He might be asleep.

Past: He must have been exhausted. (I'm sure he was exhausted then.)
Past: She must have been at work.
Past: They can't have been serious.

4. used to / would / be used to — The Trap

There are three completely different structures that all use the word used. Turkish speakers mix them up constantly because the Turkish equivalents are unrelated in form.

FormMeaningExamplesTurkish
used to + base verb A past habit or state that has now stopped I used to play basketball every Saturday.
She used to live in Izmir.
We used to go to the same school.
He used to be very shy.
There used to be a park here.		 Eskiden basketbol oynardım.

You'll get used to it eventually.		 Kaybetmeye alışıyor.
⚠ The three critical rules:
1. would cannot describe states.
❌ I would have a dog. → ✅ I used to have a dog.
❌ She would be shy. → ✅ She used to be shy.

2. used to is always past. be used to is always present or general.
✅ I used to smoke. (I smoked in the past. I don't now.)
✅ I'm used to the noise. (The noise doesn't bother me now.)

3. be used to is followed by a noun or -ing, never a base verb.
❌ I'm used to get up early. → ✅ I'm used to getting up early.
❌ She's used to work hard. → ✅ She's used to working hard.
💡 Pronounce it correctly too.
used to → /ˈjuːstə/ — the d and t blend into one sound. It sounds almost like "yoosta."
be used to → /ˈjuːzd tə/ — the d is voiced and clearly pronounced.

Say these aloud:
I used to live there. → "I yoosta liv there."
I'm used to living there. → "I'm yoozd to living there."

Seventeen, Sleep-Deprived and Sponsored

Adapted article · approx. 400 words

When Emre was fifteen, he was ranked in the top two hundred players in Europe. By seventeen, he had signed his first sponsorship deal, moved into a team house in another city, and stopped attending school regularly. His parents had agreed to it because the contract was lucrative and because Emre had promised he would continue his studies online. He never did.

The routine inside the house was meticulous. Players woke at eleven, practised from noon until six, reviewed footage until nine, then scrimmaged against foreign teams until two in the morning. Emre's coach, a former professional who had retired at twenty-three due to a wrist injury, monitored everything — sleep, diet, screen time, even the players' moods.

"For the first year, I was euphoric," Emre says. "I was doing the only thing I had ever wanted to do, and people were paying me for it." The euphoria did not last. By the second year, his reaction times had slowed fractionally, and in esports a fraction is the difference between a career and a memory. He began sleeping four hours a night. His appetite deteriorated. He stopped calling his mother because he didn't want her to hear how he sounded.

What Emre experienced has a name now. Sports psychologists call it burnout, and it is becoming alarmingly common among young esports players whose careers begin and end far earlier than in traditional sport. A footballer might peak at twenty-eight. A Counter-Strike player might be considered obsolete at twenty-four.

The industry has started to respond — slowly. A handful of major organisations now employ full-time psychologists. Some leagues have introduced mandatory rest periods between tournaments. But critics argue that these measures are cosmetic, because the underlying economics have not changed: there are thousands of teenagers willing to sacrifice their health for a chance at a career that only a few dozen will actually achieve.

Emre left the team house at nineteen. He now studies computer engineering and coaches a local amateur squad twice a week. 1. Present Perfect Simple vs. Continuous vs. Past Simple

This is the hardest tense system for Turkish speakers, because Turkish uses -dı for both I went and I have gone. English forces you to choose. The choice is about whether the time period is finished or unfinished, and whether the focus is on the result or the duration.

TenseFormFocusExamples
Past Simple verb-ed / irregular Finished time. You say when. The action is over and done. I moved to Istanbul in 2019.
I visited Ankara last summer.
She called me yesterday.
We met three years ago.
He finished the project two days ago.
Present Perfect Simple have/has + V3 Result now, or life experience. The exact time is not stated. I have moved three times. (experience)
She has just arrived. (result now)
I've never eaten sushi.
He has broken his leg. (so he can't walk now)
We have already seen that film.
Present Perfect Continuous have/has been + V-ing Duration. The action may still be continuing. Emphasis on the process. I have been living here for five years.
He has been studying Turkish for two years.
She's been waiting since 3 p.m.
They've been arguing all morning.
It's been raining for hours.
⚠ The #1 error in Turkey: using Present Perfect with a finished time expression.
❌ I have visited Ankara last summer. → ✅ I visited Ankara last summer.
❌ She has called me yesterday. → ✅ She called me yesterday.
❌ I have finished it two days ago. → ✅ I finished it two days ago.
❌ We have met in 2019. → ✅ We met in 2019.

Rule: If the sentence contains yesterday, last week, in 2019, two days ago, when I was young, on Monday, you must use Past Simple.
💡 for vs. since — the eternal confusion
for + a period of time: for three years, for six months, for a long time, for two hours, for ages, for a week
since + a point in time: since 2018, since Monday, since I was ten, since we met, since primary school, since 3 o'clock

I've lived here for five years. (period)
I've lived here since 2019. (point)
I've been waiting for two hours.
I've been waiting since 3 p.m.
She's known him for ten years.
She's known him since primary school.
💡 Present Perfect Simple or Continuous? — the choice
Simple focuses on completion or result:
I've painted the room. (It's finished. Look at it.)
She's written three books. (Result — three finished books.)

Continuous focuses on duration or process:
I've been painting the room. (I may still be painting. I'm tired.)
She's been writing all day. (Process — she's still at it.)

With stative verbs, always use the simple form:
✅ I've known her for years. ❌ I've been knowing her for years.
✅ I've had this phone for two years. ❌ I've been having this phone…
🇹🇷 More Turkish-speaker errors:
❌ She has been knowing him for ten years. → ✅ She has known him for ten years.
❌ We have live here since 2016. → ✅ We have lived here since 2016.
❌ I have never went abroad. → ✅ I have never gone abroad.
❌ I have saw that film. → ✅ I have seen that film.
❌ He has came already. → ✅ He has come already.

Two Homes, One Heart

Adapted personal essay · approx. 420 words

My name is Selin and I have been living in Vienna for four years, which is longer than I ever expected. I came here at fourteen because of my father's job, and I have spent almost every day since trying to answer a question that nobody can answer for me: where am I actually from?

The first year was the hardest. I had studied German for two years in Ankara, so I arrived believing I could manage. I could not manage. The German my teacher had taught me was slow and careful; the German in the school corridor was fast, clipped, and full of words that had never appeared in my textbook. For six months, I understood roughly half of every conversation, and I laughed at jokes about two seconds too late, which is exactly long enough for everyone to notice.

What nobody warned me about was the loneliness of being bilingual. At school, I was the Turkish girl whose German was strange. When I called my cousins in Ankara, I was the Austrian girl whose Turkish had become strange. I had two languages and belonged fully to neither. A psychologist I spoke to later told me this was completely normal, and that most people in the diaspora experience some version of it. It did not feel normal at the time.

Things changed slowly. I joined the school's volleyball team, which had nothing to do with language and everything to do with belonging. On the court, nobody cared where I was from; they cared whether I could pass the ball. I made one friend, then another. I stopped translating in my head before speaking. Somewhere in the third year, I noticed that I had stopped counting the months.

Then, last summer, I went back to Ankara for six weeks — and experienced something I had never heard of. Reverse culture shock. My old friends had moved on, with new inside jokes and new routines I was not part of. The city felt louder than I remembered. I found myself missing Vienna, which confused me deeply, because I had spent three years missing Ankara.

I have stopped trying to choose between the two. I have accepted that I am not half-Turkish and half-Austrian, as though I were a pie cut into slices. I am both, entirely, at the same time, and the two parts do not compete. My roots are in Ankara. My life is in Vienna. Neither of those facts cancels out the other.

1. Future Forms — The Full Set

Turkish has one main future tense (-ecek/-acak) and a present continuous used for arrangements. English splits the future into five forms. The choice depends on how certain you are, whether it's planned, and whether it's about a moment or a completed deadline.

FormUseExamples
will + base Predictions (no evidence), instant decisions, promises, offers AI will replace some jobs.
I'll help you with that.
I think it will rain tomorrow.
I'll call you back in five minutes.
She'll be a great doctor one day.
going to + base Plans and intentions decided before speaking, or predictions with evidence I'm going to study computer science.
Look at those clouds — it's going to rain.
We're going to move to Izmir next year.
She's going to start a YouTube channel.
Present Continuous Fixed arrangements — a time and place are already set I'm meeting a careers advisor on Friday at 3.
We're having dinner with my aunt tonight.
She's flying to Berlin on Monday.
I'm seeing the dentist at 4 p.m.
Future Continuous
will be + V-ing		 In progress at a specific future moment In 2035, I'll be working from home.
At 8 tomorrow, I'll be taking the exam.
This time next week, we'll be lying on a beach.
Don't call at 9 — I'll be studying.
Future Perfect
will have + V3		 Completed BEFORE a specific future point By 2035, I'll have finished university.
By next June, she'll have written her book.
By the time you arrive, we'll have eaten.
By 8 p.m., I'll have done all my homework.
Future Perfect Continuous
will have been + V-ing		 Duration up to a future point (rare, but impressive) By June, I'll have been studying English for six years.
By 2030, she'll have been teaching for twenty years.
Next month, we'll have been living here for a decade.
💡 Future Continuous vs. Future Perfect — the easiest way to remember
At 8 p.m. tomorrow, I will be having dinner. → I'm in the middle of it.
By 8 p.m. tomorrow, I will have had dinner. → It's finished before then.

More comparisons:
At 10 a.m. tomorrow, she'll be flying to London. (In progress.)
By 10 a.m. tomorrow, she'll have flown to London. (Completed.)

This time next year, I'll be studying medicine. (In progress.)
By next year, I'll have finished my first year of medicine. (Completed.)
⚠ Two rules that trip everyone up
1. Never use will after when, if, before, after, as soon as, until, by the time.
❌ When I will arrive, I will call you. → ✅ When I arrive, I will call you.
❌ I'll text you as soon as I will get there. → ✅ I'll text you as soon as I get there.
❌ If it will rain, we will stay home. → ✅ If it rains, we will stay home.
❌ Before you will leave, say goodbye. → ✅ Before you leave, say goodbye.

2. going to needs the full be.
❌ I going to study. → ✅ I'm going to study.
❌ We going to win. → ✅ We're going to win.
💡 The will vs. going to decision tree
Ask yourself: Did I decide this before now?
• No, I just decided → will.
"I'll have the chicken."
"I'll help you."

• Yes, I already planned it → going to.
"I'm going to have the chicken — I decided this morning."
"I'm going to study medicine."

• Yes, and I have a time and place arranged → Present Continuous.
"I'm having lunch with the coach at 1."
"I'm seeing the doctor tomorrow at 10."

⚠ The three classic errors
1. will in the if-clause.
❌ If I will study hard, I will pass. → ✅ If I study hard, I will pass.
❌ If she will come, I will be happy. → ✅ If she comes, I will be happy.

2. was instead of were.
❌ If I was you… → ✅ If I were you…
❌ If he was rich… → ✅ If he were rich…
In standard English, were is used for all persons in the Second Conditional.

3. would in the if-clause.
❌ If I would have money, I would travel. → ✅ If I had money, I would travel.
❌ If she would study, she would pass. → ✅ If she studied, she would pass.
💡 Advanced add-on — unless
Unless = if not. It replaces the if-clause entirely.
Unless you study, you'll fail. = If you don't study, you'll fail.
Unless we leave now, we'll miss the train. = If we don't leave now, we'll miss the train.

❌ Never unless you don't study — that means if you do study.

3. Passive Voice — All Tenses

Turkish uses the passive with the suffix -il/-ıl (yapıldı), so the concept is familiar. The challenge in English is that the passive must be formed with the correct form of be + the past participle (V3).

TenseActivePassiveFormula
Present SimpleThey make phones in China.Phones are made in China.am/is/are + V3
Present ContinuousThey are filming season 3.Season 3 is being filmed.am/is/are being + V3
Present PerfectThey have released the update.The update has been released.have/has been + V3
Past SimpleSomeone stole my account.My account was stolen.was/were + V3
Past ContinuousThey were filming the scene.The scene was being filmed.was/were being + V3
Past PerfectThey had cancelled the show.The show had been cancelled.had been + V3
Future (will)They will replace the system.The system will be replaced.will be + V3
Future (going to)They are going to release it.It is going to be released.going to be + V3
Modal (must)You must follow the rules.The rules must be followed.modal + be + V3

Passive with more examples
Present Simple: Turkish is spoken in Germany by millions. · Coffee is grown in Brazil. · This song is played everywhere.
Past Simple: The pyramids were built by the ancient Egyptians. · My bike was stolen last night. · The match was cancelled due to rain.
Past Continuous: The road was being repaired when we drove past. · The house was being painted all week.
Present Perfect: The app has been downloaded a million times. · My phone has been repaired. · The rules have been changed.
Future: The results will be announced tomorrow. · The new bridge is going to be opened in June.
Modal: The homework must be handed in by Friday. · This problem can be solved easily. · The report should be finished today.
Process descriptions: First, the beans are roasted. Then they are ground. Finally, hot water is added.
💡 When to use the passive — three clear situations
1. The doer is unknown:
My phone was stolen.
The window has been broken.

2. The doer is obvious or unimportant:
The suspect was arrested. (Obviously by police.)
The road is being repaired. (Obviously by workers.)

3. You want to focus on the action or result:
The film was directed by Nolan.
The novel was written in 1925.

Passive is standard in: formal writing, science reports, news headlines, and process descriptions.
⚠ The dark side of the passive — evading responsibility
"Mistakes were made." — Nobody says who made them.
"The decision was taken." — By whom?
"Your application has been rejected." — By a person? By a machine?
In persuasive writing, the passive can hide the agent. This is why journalists ask: who is the agent?

❌ These phones are make in China. → ✅ These phones are made in China.

💡 How to test if a sentence is correctly passive
Ask yourself: Is there a form of be + a past participle?
• "The book was written." → was + written → ✅ correct
• "The book was write." → was + base → ❌ wrong
• "The book written." → no be → ❌ wrong
• "The book is writing." → wrong tense → ❌ wrong (that's active continuous)

Will a Machine Take Your Job — Or Just Change It?

Adapted article · approx. 430 words

Every generation has been told that machines are coming for their jobs. Weavers were told it in 1810. Bank clerks were told it in 1970. Factory workers were told it in 1990. In each case, some jobs disappeared — and entirely new categories of work were created that nobody had imagined.

What is different this time, economists argue, is speed and scope. Previous waves of automation replaced physical labour. Artificial intelligence is beginning to replace cognitive labour: drafting, translating, summarising, diagnosing, designing. These are tasks that were, until very recently, considered safely human.

The optimistic case is that AI will not eliminate work so much as restructure it. A radiologist will not be replaced by a machine, but a radiologist who uses AI will replace one who refuses to. The skill that will matter most, according to this view, is not technical knowledge but adaptability: the willingness to upskill continuously, to abandon expertise that has become obsolete, and to learn something new every few years.

The pessimistic case is less comfortable. It points out that the gig economy is already expanding, that middle-income jobs are being hollowed out, and that the benefits of productivity gains are not automatically shared. If a company can replace forty employees with one system, the shareholder gains — but the forty employees do not. Historically, the transition periods have been brutal, sometimes lasting a generation.

There is also a question that is not economic at all. If an algorithm writes the novel, composes the song, or paints the picture, who is the author? Several lawsuits are currently testing this. In one well-publicised case, a group of artists argued that their work had been used to train an AI model without their consent or payment. The companies responded that the training data was publicly available. The outcome of these cases will shape the creative industries for decades.

What should a fifteen-year-old take from all this? Probably not panic. But possibly this: the value of a person is increasingly not in what they know, because machines will know more, but in what they can do with it — how they connect ideas, how they persuade, how they collaborate, and how they decide what is worth doing in the first place. Those capabilities are not yet automated. Whether they will be is the only question that matters.
